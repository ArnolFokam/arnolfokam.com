---
title: "The Lost Art of Knowing What the H*ck is Going On!"
date: "2026-11-10"
dek: "An attempt to rediscover first principles and understand the hidden mechanisms governing the systems we build, use and interact with."
---

I recently interviewed for a research engineering position at a BioAI startup here in London, and I have been thinking about how I felt ever since I walked out of their offices.

The last time I felt this way was during my first pair programming session with a good friend of mine called Michael, one of the best engineers I have ever met. That encounter helped me appreciate the different levels of systems thinking.

In a way, this made me a little angry at myself. Why? I realised that, over the years, I had slowly outsourced my systems thinking to a probabilistic people-pleaser (a.k.a. an LLM). Slowly but surely, I had started confusing “getting things done” with “knowing how things are done”. The green check marks ✅ next to AI-generated test cases gradually increased my confidence in a black box, and my work started to feel more like alchemy than careful engineering.

Don’t get me wrong. I believe AI research involves a little bit of art, almost something mysterious. Those lucky enough to develop the right intuition about the field can appear like “magicians” to the average research engineer.

However, I wonder whether I have become a little too comfortable with the spectacle offered by my Anthropic-powered Wizard of Oz, while paying very little attention to what is happening behind the curtain.

Another realisation I have had while writing this piece is that, if I do not go against the current trend, I may soon find myself unable to understand the layers of knowledge behind the carefully designed abstractions created by my contemporaries or, even worse, by AI agents.

This is a fate I cannot allow myself to fall into. This brings me to a personal belief that I want to share with you:

> Life can be thought of as a partially observable dynamical system. At any given moment, we only have access to a limited set of observations, from which we must infer the underlying state of the world. The quality of our decisions therefore depends partly on the quality of the internal models we build to understand the hidden factors that shape the systems we interact with. ***We build these internal models through abstractions, some of which we create ourselves and others we inherit from previous generations of humans.***

However, the cure can also become the plague. Why do I say that?

Abstractions serve several purposes in life:

- Simplifying decision-making.
- Filtering out unnecessary information.
- Creating a common language for collaboration.
- And so on.

But here is where they can become a problem: abstractions carry the biases of the people who design them. As we add more layers of abstraction, errors in our judgement can build up at each layer we use to understand a system.

And this is where the tension lies: how deep should we go? I recognise that it is not practical to study every system from its most basic building blocks. But as an engineer, I have learned to appreciate the beauty of simply “knowing how things work”, even when doing so brings no direct benefit in terms of income or status.

Anyway, enough ranting! I have always been a person of action, and this is my pledge:

> I cannot hope to break down all the abstractions that exist in life. But I, Arnol Manuel Fokam, pledge to explore the following layers of abstraction in deep learning with an almost religious level of curiosity:
>
> Evaluation Metrics → Learning Objectives → Model Architecture → Representations → Learning Algorithms → Numerical Optimisation → Mathematical Foundations → Computational Foundations → Physical Foundations of Computation.

Hopefully, I will be able to document this exciting journey and share it with you guys, although I cannot promise regular updates.

Until next time…
